Mill-ionaire is a letter shop tycoon built around a simulated vertical knee mill, the kind of manual machine a Bridgeport is. You cut metal letters, fill customer word orders, buy new finishes and more mills, and grow one machine into a factory floor. It is made by Mohave Technology Group.

Play it in a browser at mill-ionaire.vercel.app. Nothing installs. A downloaded copy runs the same way: unzip it and open index.html in any web browser, with or without internet.

How to play. Customers post word orders on the orders tab. Each word has to be made from letters cut in one finish, like brass or blue anodize, and shipping it pays cash. On the mill tab, tap run the order program under the machine. The mill loads a blank in the right finish, cuts whatever letter the orders are missing, and drops it straight into the bins. Tap it again to stop it. Next to it, cut the whole alphabet runs the mill through every letter in order, and call the millwright repairs a wrecked machine. You can also cut by hand with the wheels and sliders, then bag the part and stow it in its bin. A flashing marker shows a new player what to tap next until the first floor mill is running.

The orders tab is the shipping room. Ship an order when every tile has a blue corner. Loose parts in the tray only count once they are stowed, and the stow button puts them all away at once. The empty the bins button asks before it scraps anything.

Words get longer as your shop grows, and longer words pay more for every letter. Once you run several mills the spelling bee sends in long words at a premium, and now and then a collector wants a whole matching set of the alphabet, the numbers or the emoji, which pays double. Once you own a floor mill, a caller on the phone sometimes says the word out loud instead of writing it down. The order card shows only a speaker button and an empty slot for every letter. Tap hear it to listen again, tap spell it to type the word on the letter keys, and once every slot is filled the order program cuts any of those letters the bins are missing. Tap sell when the tiles are ready. Spelled right, a spoken word pays more than any other word. Spelled wrong, the customer docks you half the order price, your letters stay in the bins and the slots clear so you can try again. Words that sound like other words are never spoken. Number dies and emoji dies in the shop unlock house number orders and emoji orders, and emoji are the best paying parts there are. The engine lathe in the shop costs half the price of your next mill and gets its own lathe tab. It turns round parts from bar stock in any finish: sign standoffs, threaded studs, sign posts, bushings, flanges, lazy susan plates, knobs and fixture pins. Once you own it, customers order batches of turned parts, words mounted on a standoff for every letter, and signs on posts, so the mill and the lathe have to work together. The lathe program turns whatever the orders need straight into the bins, and when nothing is on order it makes the part you picked on the lathe tab to stock, until that shelf is full. Standoffs and studs are open from the start, and every turned part you make counts toward unlocking the next part on the catalog, all the way up to lazy susan plates. To cut by hand, load a bar, start the spindle, feed the cross slide in and run the carriage along the bar, then part it off, and a piece that matches a part on the catalog goes to the tray.

Closing the game is a power outage. When it reopens, every floor machine that was running comes back with its emergency stop tripped, the way a real machine does when the power drops, and somebody on the crew has to walk over, reset it and run the boot cycle before it cuts again. Your own mill, the lathe and the brake run a short boot cycle and then their programs start back up on their own. Any machine that sits still for a while, for any reason, gets a visit from whoever on the crew is free, and they say what they found in a bubble over their head on the floor tab: this one ain't got no gas in it, somebody switched this one off, nothing on order it can make, ain't got money for stock, or the shelf is out of the spare part it needs. While they work they say what they are fixing too. Lasers also run out of assist gas now and an operator swaps the tank.

The button at the top of the floor tab, every machine works the order queue, puts the whole shop on the orders in one tap. It sets every floor machine to the queue and starts it, and turns on your own mill's order program, the lathe program and the brake program, and it shows a check while everything is on. Each floor machine also has two buttons, work the order queue and make one part, with the picked one in blue, and a line saying what it is doing and what that kind of machine can make, so an idle machine says why it is idle. Statistics notes when orders need formed or turned parts and nothing that makes them is running.

The order program on your own mill stays on until you stop it, and it starts back up when you reopen the game. When every open order is covered, it and the floor mills on auto cut ahead in the newest finish, a few of each letter, so the bins are stocked for whatever comes in next. A machine that stops for a tool change, stock or a repair lets go of the letter it was cutting so another machine can take it. In the warehouse, bar feeders load three times the stock and the crew moves faster across the bigger floor, and when a breakdown needs a trade you have not hired, an operator covers it at a third of the speed. The ship every ready order button at the top of the orders tab ships every finished order in one tap.

The stamping press, the powder coat booth and the LED wiring bench open orders that need a finishing step. Those orders go down the finishing line on the orders tab after you ship them, each station working its own queue, and the customer pays more when they come off.

Spend your cash in the shop. New finishes and textures pay more per letter but are harder metal, so they cut slower. Floor mills run on their own on the floor tab, and your own mill sits in the first bay so you can see how much room is left. Tap a floor mill to set its letter and finish, switch it between auto and manual, or stop it on its own. When a light turns amber the mill needs a hand, so tap it to send an operator. The foreman sends operators without being told. A rush shift puts a timed quota on the board for a cash bonus.

Once you own a floor mill, your operators draw wages every minute, and every mill draws power while it cuts. The shop shows what the business costs to run each minute. If the cash runs dry the power gets cut and the crew goes home until you ship an order. The chip conveyor, better coolant, better cutters and better software make every mill run harder for the same bills, and the high speed head makes your own mill cut faster.

The press brake in the shop takes a floor bay and gets its own brake tab. It folds sheet into shape one bend at a time: angle brackets and mounting brackets first, then U channel, hat channel and sign box frames, and finally the giant chip, taco, donut and the muffler man. The brake program forms whatever the orders need or the part you picked. By hand, you hold press and let go when the bend reaches its angle, and holding too long overbends it into scrap. With the brake running, customers order batches of formed parts and big storefront signs for donut shops, taco stands, grocery stores and muffler shops. A big sign needs its letters, a box frame, brackets, standoffs from the lathe and sometimes a giant shape on top, and it pays the most of anything in the game.

The five axis CNC is the expensive shortcut to a simpler shop. A CNC takes a floor bay and cuts letters, numbers, emoji and turned parts in one machine, faster than a knee mill, with no chip stops, half the tool wear and a smaller power bill, on stock that costs a little more per part. The shop can also make your own mill a five axis CNC, which still runs free when the cash is gone and turns parts on the order program, and can swap the lathe for a CNC in the same bay, so a rich shop can fill its floor with nothing but CNCs.

Filament printers are the plastic side of the shop. A filament printer in the shop takes a floor bay and prints letters, numbers and emoji in white, black, red, blue or yellow filament, the color of the spool it has loaded, and owning one brings in orders in those colors that only a printer can make. On the floor it draws the part rising off a gold bed one layer at a time with the spool hanging on the side. A printer builds in layers, so a thin 1 is quick and a fat 8 or an emoji takes a while. It works the orders in the color already on its spool before anything that needs a swap, and a different color means an operator walks over and changes the spool, so a printer kept on one color wastes the least time. Now and then a print lets go of the bed and turns to spaghetti, the plastic is wasted and the foreman sends someone to scrape it off. Tap a printer to pick its spool color and use its keypad to print any part ahead for the bins. Orders take from the bins first, so an order for something already printed in the right color is ready to ship before anything goes to a printer.

The warehouse is the step past a floor full of CNCs. Buying it in the shop moves every machine and the whole crew into a building with three times the floor space. The floor tab draws the warehouse smaller so every bay fits on screen, with simpler machines and the crew as dots colored by trade. In the warehouse, orders start coming by the flatbed truckload. One word is spelled out on the trailer with a stack under every letter for the copies it carries, the order program and the floor cut the whole load, and shipping the truck pays for all of it at a bulk premium while the truck drives off the screen. Bigger machines also break in new ways, and each kind of breakdown needs its own trade. Operators still clear chips, change tools and load stock. Millwrights replace stripped drive gears and worn ballscrew nuts, electricians reset faulted spindle drives and tripped breakers, and controls techs clear CNC probe alarms and replace crashed tool holders. Gears, nuts and tool holders come off the spare parts shelf, and only a five axis CNC can make them. A CNC on auto makes the spare a broken machine is waiting on first, then the orders, then tops up the shelf when it has nothing else to do. A CNC on manual can be set to make any spare. The foreman sends the right trade to every breakdown, and the floor tab shows the crew and the shelf under the bays.

The office building runs the business side. It is expensive to buy and costs money every minute to keep open, and it has to be bought before you can hire sales managers and linguistics engineers. A sales manager ships any order on their own the moment its parts are in the bins, one order every few seconds. A linguistics engineer takes the spoken orders, spells each word right so the floor can cut its letters, and sells it as soon as they are in. Both are hired for a big price and draw a big salary every minute, and each one only handles so many orders, so a shop with a backlog hires a second or a third. The orders tab shows a line for the office with how many orders are waiting on each. When the cash runs out the office goes dark along with the floor until the bills are paid.

Past the CNCs, the warehouse sells two more kinds of machines, each taking a floor bay. The laser cutter cuts gusset plates, enclosure panels, logo cutouts and way guards. The metal 3D printer slowly prints rocket nozzles, injector plates, impellers and coolant manifolds. Owning them brings laser cut batches, printed batches and rocket engine kits that spell a word like THRUST or LIFTOFF alongside a printed nozzle, an injector plate and laser cut gussets, and the kits pay double. A laser and printer tech keeps them running by cleaning lenses, clearing powder jams and replacing burnt nozzle tips and damaged recoater blades.

Every kind of machine makes repair parts for the others. CNCs make drive gears, ballscrew nuts and tool holders, the laser cuts recoater blades for the printers, and the printer makes nozzle tips for the lasers. Making a spare costs the metal and some machine time. When the shelf is empty and a machine breaks, the part gets bought from the dealer at a steep markup if there is cash for it, so a varied floor pays for itself.

The end of the game is the Bridgeport mill. Once the floor has a CNC, a laser and a printer, plus a lathe or a CNC for the leadscrews, customers order whole Bridgeport mills. A Bridgeport needs leadscrews, a saddle casting, a knee casting and a mill head off the CNCs, way guards off the laser and a coolant manifold off the printer, and selling one pays more than anything else in the game. The first one out the door makes you a real Mill-ionaire.

The office grows too. The office building has a handful of seats, the office unit doubles them and the office tower doubles them again, and every step up costs more to run. Seats are shared by everyone on the business side. Besides sales managers and linguistics engineers there are finance analysts, who bring back a return on the cash most minutes but now and then lose a chunk when a customer on credit goes bankrupt, and designers, who invent designer textures one at a time: two tone blue and green, candy cane, gift wrap, jingle bells and halloween. Designer letters pay well but cut slow on expensive stock, so they are a fun way to make thinner margins.

The outside view button on the floor tab shows the business from the street: the shop or the warehouse with the name on the front in blue, the office next door at whatever size you have built, smoke from the stack while the floor runs, and flatbed trucks rolling past with the words from your open orders spelled out in giant letters.

Raw stock costs money. Every mill blank and every lathe bar is bought in the finish being cut, harder finishes cost more, and the shop lists each finish's blank and bar price. When the cash runs out, your own mill keeps running on scrap rack stock and free power in any finish, so a shop left running over lunch keeps cutting and shipping its way back while the floor waits for the bills to be paid.

When you are done, open the menu and tap I'm done to cash out. Your score is every dollar the business ever earned, from a few hundred up into the quadrillions, and it goes on the shared high score board under a tag of up to three letters, with an optional link to your own site that shows under your tag on the board. The score service checks every link against malware block lists and a slur filter before it takes it. Submitting retires the business and the next one starts fresh. High scores in the menu shows the board at any time. Scores live on the shared score service in the sudosilo 2026-10-30-orange-scores repo, where Mill-ionaire is registered.

Statistics in the menu opens the books. It shows the net worth of the company, counting cash, the machines, buildings and upgrades at resale, and the parts in the bins. It breaks income down by orders, the finance team and bonuses, and costs down by raw stock, spare parts bought from the dealer, floor crew wages, office rent and salaries, power and shop purchases, with the running cost per minute and the operating margin. It counts orders shipped by kind, shows how many machines are cutting, waiting on a hand or idle, and draws a chart of money in and money out each minute over the shift. Under the chart, notes point at what to fix first, like machines waiting on spares, orders waiting to ship, idle machines or an office that costs more than it earns.

The market cools off the longer you run the shop, so every letter pays a little less as the hours go by. Early money is the easiest money you will ever make.

The shop makes sound. Each running mill hums, chatters while it cuts, and squeals once in a while, and a full floor sounds like a whole shop. The blue note button turns sound on and off, and the menu has the shop volume. The menu also holds how to play and starting the whole business over, which asks before it throws anything away. The blue i button opens the same how to play and information panel.

The machine. X traverses the table left and right, Y moves the saddle in and out, and Z raises and lowers the whole knee so the work comes up to the cutter. A tilts the head over for angled work. The big view is a cutaway taken straight through the part at whatever Y the saddle is sitting at, and the stock panel shows the same part from above.

What is real and what is rough. The part is solid material, so you can machine one face, unclamp it, turn it over and machine another. Cut past the bottom of the work and you eat the table, and cut right through and you part the leadscrew until the millwright rebuilds it. The feed rate a cutter will take depends on spindle speed and on how hard the finish is. Prices, wages and the market are a game economy, not real shop rates.

How it looks. Every machine is drawn as a shop manual illustration: flat color, one dark outline, a highlight on the castings and a soft shadow on the floor. The knee mill shows its column, ram, motor, knee and table, a five axis CNC is an enclosed cabinet with a window, a blue control pendant and a stack light, and the lathe, the press brake, the laser with its gas bottle and the metal printer each have their own picture. The same drawings are used on the floor, shrunk down for the warehouse, and as small pictures beside each thing the shop sells, so a machine is never mistaken for a person. The crew are little figures in coveralls the color of their trade with white hard hats. Bays are painted slabs with amber safety corners that turn rust red when a machine needs a hand. Your own mill turns into an enclosed CNC with a pendant that shows the letter being cut once you upgrade it. Buttons look pressable, the cash and market gauges sit in one tight row, and switching tabs starts the new tab at its top. The whole game stays on a light putty background with shop blue details.

How it is built. The whole game is mill-ionaire-v2.html, a single file with canvas drawing, plain JavaScript and sound made in the browser, with no downloads and no server. Progress saves in the browser on the device that plays it. The vercel.json file serves the game at the site root. Every push to GitHub runs mill-ionaire-v2-test.js, which plays the whole game in a headless browser, and when every check passes it builds the itch zip and posts it on the itch-latest release.

The thinking behind it. Every item is a letter, every machine turns blanks into letters, every finish changes what a letter is worth, and every order asks for a set of them. Grow the shop the way a real one grows, one more machine at a time.
