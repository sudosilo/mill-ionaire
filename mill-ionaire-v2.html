how to play

Customers post word orders on the orders tab. Each word has to be made from letters cut in one finish, like brass or blue anodize, and shipping it pays cash.

On the mill tab, tap run the order program under the machine. The mill loads a blank in the right finish, cuts whatever letter the orders are missing, and drops it straight into the bins. You can also cut by hand with the wheels and sliders, then bag the part and drag it into its bin.

Words get longer as your shop grows, and longer words pay more for every letter. Once you run several mills the spelling bee sends in long words at a premium, and now and then a collector wants a whole matching set, which pays double. Number dies and emoji dies in the shop unlock house numbers and emoji, and emoji are the best paying parts there are.

The engine lathe in the shop costs half the price of your next mill. It turns round parts: sign standoffs, threaded studs, sign posts, bushings, flanges, lazy susan plates, knobs and fixture pins. Once you own it, customers order batches of turned parts, words mounted on standoffs and signs on posts. Run the lathe program on the lathe tab and it turns whatever the orders need, and when nothing is on order it makes the part you picked to stock. Standoffs and studs come first, and every turned part you make counts toward unlocking the next part on the catalog.

The press brake in the shop takes a floor bay and folds sheet into brackets, channel and sign box frames, and later giant chips, tacos, donuts and the muffler man. With the brake running, customers order big storefront signs that need letters, a box frame, brackets, standoffs from the lathe and sometimes a giant shape on top, and those pay the most of anything.

Raw stock costs money. Every mill blank and every lathe bar is bought in the finish you cut, and harder finishes cost more. The shop shows each finish's blank and bar price. If the cash runs out, your own mill keeps running on scrap rack stock and free power, so a shop left running over lunch keeps working while the floor waits for the bills to be paid.

The stamping press, the powder coat booth and the LED wiring bench open orders that need a finishing step. Those orders go down the finishing line on the orders tab after you ship them, and the customer pays more when they come off.

Spend your cash in the shop. New finishes pay more per letter but are harder metal, so they cut slower. Floor mills run on their own on the floor tab, and operators keep them going when a light turns amber.

Once you own a floor mill, your operators draw wages every minute, and every running mill draws power. If the cash runs dry the power gets cut and the crew goes home until you ship an order. Chip conveyors, better coolant, better cutters and better software in the shop make every mill run harder for the same bills.

The market cools off the longer you run the shop, so every letter pays a little less as the hours go by. Early money is the easiest money you will ever make.

what it does

Mill-ionaire is a letter shop tycoon built around a simulated vertical knee mill, the kind of manual machine a Bridgeport is. You cut metal letters, fill customer word orders, buy new finishes and more mills, and grow one machine into a factory floor.

X traverses the table left and right, Y moves the saddle in and out, Z raises and lowers the whole knee so the work comes up to the cutter. A tilts the head over for angled work. The big view is a cutaway taken straight through the part at whatever Y the saddle is sitting at, and the stock panel is the same part seen from above.

what is real and what is rough

The part is solid material, so you can machine one face, unclamp it, turn it over and machine another. Cut past the bottom of the work and you eat the table, and cut right through and you part the leadscrew until the millwright rebuilds it. The feed rate a cutter will take is rationed by spindle speed and by how hard the finish is. Prices and the market are a game economy, not real shop rates.

the thinking behind it

Every item is a letter, every machine turns blanks into letters, every finish changes what a letter is worth, and every order asks for a set of them. Grow the shop the way a real one grows, one more machine at a time.

Floor mills run on their own. On auto they cut whatever the open orders are missing and put it straight in the bins. Tap a mill to set it by hand. When a light goes amber the mill has stopped and needs a hand, so tap it to send the next free operator over. A foreman from the shop sends operators without being told. A rush shift puts a timed quota on the board for a cash bonus.

The lathe spins the bar and the tool cuts it from the side. Run the lathe program and it turns whatever the orders need: sign standoffs, threaded studs, sign posts, bushings, flanges, lazy susan plates, knobs and fixture pins, straight into the bins. To cut by hand, load a bar, start the spindle, slide the cross slide in to take a cut and run the carriage along the bar. Stop the spindle with the carriage where the part should end and part it off. If the shape matches a part on the catalog it goes to the tray, and if it does not it goes in the scrap bin.

press brake, side view of the bend
load a sheet, then hold press to bend by hand, or run the brake program

The press brake folds flat sheet into shape one bend at a time. Run the brake program and it forms whatever the orders need, and when nothing is on order it forms the part you picked, straight into the bins. To bend by hand, pick a part, load a sheet, and hold press. Let go when the bend reaches its angle. Let go early and you can press again, but hold too long and you overbend it into scrap. Angle brackets and mounting brackets come first, and every formed part counts toward unlocking the next: U channel, hat channel, sign box frames, then the giant chip, taco, donut and the muffler man.

Power feed drives the table on its own at the set rate, the way the longitudinal feed box does on a real Bridgeport. Flip the switch on, pick a direction, and the table traverses until you shut it off or it runs out of travel, which is how you get an even finish across a face instead of the wobble you get cranking by hand. It respects the same crash rules, so with the spindle stopped it stops dead against the part instead of driving through it.
