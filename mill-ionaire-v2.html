how to play

Customers post word orders on the orders tab. Each word has to be made from letters cut in one finish, like brass or blue anodize, and shipping it pays cash.

On the mill tab, tap run the order program under the machine. The mill loads a blank in the right finish, cuts whatever letter the orders are missing, and drops it straight into the bins. You can also cut by hand with the wheels and sliders, then bag the part and drag it into its bin.

Words get longer as your shop grows, and longer words pay more for every letter. Once you run several mills the spelling bee sends in long words at a premium, and now and then a collector wants a whole matching set, which pays double. Once you own a floor mill, a caller on the phone sometimes says a word out loud instead of writing it down. Tap hear it, tap spell it and type the word into its slots, and the order program cuts the letters you typed. Sell it when the slots are filled. Spell it right and it pays more than any other word, spell it wrong and the customer docks you half the price. Number dies and emoji dies in the shop unlock house numbers and emoji, and emoji are the best paying parts there are.

The engine lathe in the shop costs half the price of your next mill. It turns round parts: sign standoffs, threaded studs, sign posts, bushings, flanges, lazy susan plates, knobs and fixture pins. Once you own it, customers order batches of turned parts, words mounted on standoffs and signs on posts. Run the lathe program on the lathe tab and it turns whatever the orders need, and when nothing is on order it makes the part you picked to stock. Standoffs and studs come first, and every turned part you make counts toward unlocking the next part on the catalog.

The office building in the shop houses the business side, and it costs money every minute to keep open. Inside it you can hire sales managers, who ship every order the moment its parts are in the bins, and linguistics engineers, who take the spoken orders, spell each word right so the floor can cut it, and sell it when the letters are in. Each one handles one order every few seconds and draws a big salary, so a busy shop with a backlog may need more than one.

In the warehouse the shop sells a laser cutter and a metal 3D printer, each taking a floor bay. The laser cuts gusset plates, enclosure panels, logo cutouts and way guards, and the printer slowly prints rocket nozzles, injector plates, impellers and coolant manifolds. They bring laser and printer orders and rocket engine kits that pay double, and a laser and printer tech to keep them running. Every kind of machine makes spare parts for the others: CNCs make drive gears, ballscrew nuts and tool holders, the laser cuts recoater blades for the printers, and the printer makes nozzle tips for the lasers. When the shelf is empty the dealer sells the part at a steep markup. With a CNC, a laser, a printer and a lathe or a CNC for leadscrews, customers start ordering whole Bridgeport mills, the best money in the game. The office moves up to an office unit and then an office tower for more seats, and besides sales and linguistics it seats finance analysts, who earn a return on the cash but sometimes lose a chunk to a customer going bankrupt, and designers, who invent holiday and two tone textures that pay well but cut slow on pricey stock. The outside view button on the floor tab shows the buildings from the street.

When the game reopens, the power has dropped and come back like it does in a real shop. Every floor machine that was running trips its emergency stop, and the crew walks over and resets each one with a boot cycle. Your own mill, the lathe and the brake run a boot cycle and then their programs pick back up on their own. Any machine that sits still for a while gets looked at by whoever is free, and the answer shows in a bubble over their head on the floor tab, like this one ain't got no gas in it, nothing on order it can make, or the shelf is out of the spare it needs. The crew also says what they are fixing while they work.

Every machine works the order queue, the button at the top of the floor tab, puts the whole shop on the orders in one tap: every floor machine, your own mill, the lathe program and the brake program. Tap a floor machine to pick between work the order queue and make one part, and the picked one shows blue with a check. The line under it says what the machine is doing and what it can make.

The order program on your own mill stays on until you stop it, even across closing the game, and when every order is covered it cuts ahead in your newest finish so the bins are stocked for the next orders. Floor mills on auto do the same. A machine stopped and waiting for help never holds up a letter, another machine picks it up. When a breakdown needs a trade you have not hired, an operator covers it at a third of the speed. The ship every ready order button at the top of the orders tab ships everything that is ready in one tap.

The warehouse in the shop is the next step past the CNCs. It has three times the floor space, and every machine and the whole crew move over. The floor is drawn smaller so it all fits, and the crew show up as dots colored by trade. Orders start coming by the flatbed truckload, one word spelled out over and over on the trailer, and shipping the truck pays for the whole load. Bigger machines break in new ways. Millwrights replace drive gears and ballscrew nuts, electricians reset drives and breakers, and controls techs clear CNC alarms and replace crashed tool holders. Gears, nuts and tool holders come off the spare shelf, and only a five axis CNC can make them, so CNCs on auto make spares whenever the shelf runs low or a machine is waiting on one.

The press brake in the shop takes a floor bay and folds sheet into brackets, channel and sign box frames, and later giant chips, tacos, donuts and the muffler man. With the brake running, customers order big storefront signs that need letters, a box frame, brackets, standoffs from the lathe and sometimes a giant shape on top, and those pay the most of anything.

The five axis CNC is the expensive shortcut. A CNC takes a floor bay and cuts letters, numbers, emoji and turned parts, all in one machine. It runs faster than a knee mill with no chip stops, half the tool wear and a smaller power bill, on stock that costs a little more per part. You can also make your own mill a five axis CNC, which still runs free when the cash is gone, and swap the lathe for a CNC in the same bay.

Raw stock costs money. Every mill blank and every lathe bar is bought in the finish you cut, and harder finishes cost more. The shop shows each finish's blank and bar price. If the cash runs out, your own mill keeps running on scrap rack stock and free power, so a shop left running over lunch keeps working while the floor waits for the bills to be paid.

The stamping press, the powder coat booth and the LED wiring bench open orders that need a finishing step. Those orders go down the finishing line on the orders tab after you ship them, and the customer pays more when they come off.

Spend your cash in the shop. New finishes pay more per letter but are harder metal, so they cut slower. Floor mills run on their own on the floor tab, and operators keep them going when a light turns amber.

Once you own a floor mill, your operators draw wages every minute, and every running mill draws power. If the cash runs dry the power gets cut and the crew goes home until you ship an order. Chip conveyors, better coolant, better cutters and better software in the shop make every mill run harder for the same bills.

When you are done, open the menu and tap I'm done to cash out. Your score is every dollar the business ever earned, and it goes on the shared high score board under a tag of up to three letters, with a link to your own site if you want one. Submitting retires the business, and the next one starts fresh. High scores in the menu shows the board any time. Statistics in the menu opens the books: net worth, income and costs broken down by stock, spares, crew, office and power, orders shipped by kind, how busy the floor is, a chart of money in and out each minute, and notes on what to fix first.

The market cools off the longer you run the shop, so every letter pays a little less as the hours go by. Early money is the easiest money you will ever make.

what it does

Mill-ionaire is a letter shop tycoon built around a simulated vertical knee mill, the kind of manual machine a Bridgeport is. You cut metal letters, fill customer word orders, buy new finishes and more mills, and grow one machine into a factory floor.

X traverses the table left and right, Y moves the saddle in and out, Z raises and lowers the whole knee so the work comes up to the cutter. A tilts the head over for angled work. The big view is a cutaway taken straight through the part at whatever Y the saddle is sitting at, and the stock panel is the same part seen from above.

what is real and what is rough

The part is solid material, so you can machine one face, unclamp it, turn it over and machine another. Cut past the bottom of the work and you eat the table, and cut right through and you part the leadscrew until the millwright rebuilds it. The feed rate a cutter will take is rationed by spindle speed and by how hard the finish is. Prices and the market are a game economy, not real shop rates.

how it is built

One HTML file with canvas drawing and plain JavaScript, no downloads and no server. Progress saves in the browser on this device.

the thinking behind it

Every item is a letter, every machine turns blanks into letters, every finish changes what a letter is worth, and every order asks for a set of them. Grow the shop the way a real one grows, one more machine at a time.

The lathe spins the bar and the tool cuts it from the side. Run the lathe program and it turns whatever the orders need: sign standoffs, threaded studs, sign posts, bushings, flanges, lazy susan plates, knobs and fixture pins, straight into the bins. To cut by hand, load a bar, start the spindle, slide the cross slide in to take a cut and run the carriage along the bar. Stop the spindle with the carriage where the part should end and part it off. If the shape matches a part on the catalog it goes to the tray, and if it does not it goes in the scrap bin.

press brake, side view of the bend
load a sheet, then hold press to bend by hand, or run the brake program

The press brake folds flat sheet into shape one bend at a time. Run the brake program and it forms whatever the orders need, and when nothing is on order it forms the part you picked, straight into the bins. To bend by hand, pick a part, load a sheet, and hold press. Let go when the bend reaches its angle. Let go early and you can press again, but hold too long and you overbend it into scrap. Angle brackets and mounting brackets come first, and every formed part counts toward unlocking the next: U channel, hat channel, sign box frames, then the giant chip, taco, donut and the muffler man.

Power feed drives the table on its own at the set rate, the way the longitudinal feed box does on a real Bridgeport. Flip the switch on, pick a direction, and the table traverses until you shut it off or it runs out of travel, which is how you get an even finish across a face instead of the wobble you get cranking by hand. It respects the same crash rules, so with the spindle stopped it stops dead against the part instead of driving through it.
